Between April 2024 and 2026 I completed nine modules and a dissertation while leading two DevOps teams in the securities trading division of a Swiss bank. This post looks back on the programme as a whole, using Rolfe et al.'s (2001) *What? So what? Now what?* model. The module posts cover the individual units in detail.

## What?

I started the MSc as a practitioner who knew how enterprise IT works in one organisation and wanted to understand why it works that way. The early modules gave me vocabulary and frameworks I had used without naming them: ITIL and CMDBs in IT Service Management, threat modelling and quantitative risk in Security and Risk Management, and agile and plan-driven approaches in IT Project Management. Later modules on data science, cloud and research methods moved from describing systems to analysing them with evidence.

My results tell a story too. My strongest marks came where I could connect theory to my own environment, such as the executive summary in Security and Risk Management (85%), the group report in IT Project Management (83%) and the enterprise data strategy in Into Data Science (85%). My weakest came from assignments that required technical analysis outside my daily work, such as the CMDB design (62%) and the London traffic data report (60%). <!-- CHECK: does this explanation match how you see those results? -->

The dissertation brought the threads together. It used ITSM and CMDB data, which I had first modelled in Module 3, a quantitative ranking approach that grew out of the risk modelling in Module 2, and the research design and statistics from Module 7, to prioritise technical debt in trading IT without access to source code.

## So what?

Three things changed in how I work.

**I think in evidence, not in anecdotes.** Before the programme, conversations about which legacy system to fix first were mostly opinion. The dissertation showed me that the operational data we already collect can support those decisions, as long as the limits of that data are stated honestly. The most valuable lesson was that defining what a metric means is harder, and more important, than computing it.

**I take uncertainty seriously.** In the dissertation, SAW, VIKOR and TOPSIS disagreed about the top-ranked service in the 2024 data. Earlier in my career I would have treated that as noise. Instead it became one of the most useful findings, because it showed where a ranking stops and a management judgement starts. I now try to make that boundary explicit when presenting data to stakeholders.

**I see more clearly how my role connects to governance.** The modules on risk, compliance and service management made me understand how regulatory requirements such as FINMA's on ICT risk translate into everyday operational choices like patching, lifecycle management and incident handling. As a team lead, that helps me explain why some unglamorous work matters.

Not everything went smoothly. Balancing study, work and personal life during major project phases was a real challenge.
